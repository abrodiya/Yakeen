Yakeenयक़ीन · trust, made certain UX & Service Design Case · Tier 3 India

Get the help you need,
keep the trust that’s yours.

Yakeen is a delegated-access system that lets a trusted person finish a digital task for someone who can’t, a form, a booking, a scheme, without ever seeing their password or OTP. Reachable by voice call, WhatsApp, a neighbourhood kiosk, or the app.

The case in one screen

Separating the help from the handover.

01 Problem

People who can’t finish a digital task alone are made to hand over their phone, password, and OTP to a helper or agent, trading security and autonomy for basic access.

02 Insight

People trust a helper to help, not to hold their credentials. Delegated payments are already solved (UPI Circle); the unmet need is every non-payment task that still forces a credential to change hands.

03 Approach

A voice-first, multi-channel co-pilot where a trusted helper does the task while the beneficiary watches live, and the secret step stays private through a scoped, revocable grant. The helper never sees the OTP.

04 Impact

Measured against one North-Star, tasks completed without the credential leaving the beneficiary, with a hard guardrail on credential exposure. Targets, not results: this is a concept.

What the field work taught us AI-clustered · human-reviewed

Three modes of help

Field ethnography shows help isn't one act, it's surrogate ("do it for me"), enabling ("do it with me"), and translation ("tell me what it says"). The system must support all three.

Voice is a mandate

Text-only vernacular apps were rejected in the field, people don't type. Voice input, spoken steps, and regional language aren't features; they're the price of entry.

Autonomy must be protected

Shared devices can enable monitoring and control, especially over women. So device-sharing and credential-sharing must be separated, and undo / revoke must be first-class.

02
02Why this doesn’t exist yet

Delegation is solved, for money.
Not for everything else.

The obvious question first: if letting someone act for you is useful, hasn’t it been built? For payments, yes, and well. The gap Yakeen works in is every non-payment task that still forces a password or OTP to change hands.

The benchmark
NPCI’s UPI Circle already nails delegated payments.

Since 2024, a primary user can authorise up to five trusted secondary users to pay from their account, full delegation, or partial where the primary approves each transaction with their PIN, without ever sharing the underlying credentials. By 2026 it is mainstream across BHIM, PhonePe, Google Pay and Paytm. It is a proven, regulator-blessed template for trusted delegation, and Yakeen deliberately borrows its spirit: scoped, revocable, primary stays in control.

What still isn’t covered

The gap Non-payment tasks
  • Filling a scheme or benefit form
  • Completing KYC or re-verification
  • Booking an appointment or ticket
  • Recovering a locked account
  • Reading and acting on an official notice

UPI Circle moves money. None of these move money, so they fall entirely outside its rails, and the only “solution” today is to hand over the phone and the OTP.

What people do instead
  • Surrender the credential, give the helper the phone, password, and OTP
  • Agent at a kiosk, a CSC or shop operator does it, often seeing everything
  • Wait for a relative, delay the task until someone trusted visits

Each “works,” and each trades away security, privacy, or autonomy, the exact cost this project set out to remove.

Where Yakeen sits

Take the consent model that made UPI Circle safe for payments, scoped, time-boxed, revocable, primary-in-control, and extend it to the assisted, non-payment tasks that still have no safe path. Same trust primitive; a different, unserved job.

08Discussion guide

Semi-structured interview
60 minutes.

Used in discovery, in the participant's language, with a warm-up before anything sensitive. Autonomy and safety questions are worded carefully to avoid implying distrust of family.

Warm-up & context8 min
  • Tell me about the phone you use, is it yours, or shared?
  • What do you use it for most in a normal week?
  • When was the last time you needed to do something official on it?
The task & the struggle10 min
  • Walk me through the last form, booking, or scheme you dealt with.
  • Where did you get stuck? What did that feel like?
  • What happens when you can't finish it yourself?
Who helps, and how10 min
  • Who do you turn to? Why that person?
  • Do they do it for you, with you, or just explain it?
  • What do you have to give them for it to work?
Control & autonomy12 min · word carefully
  • If a helper could finish it without seeing your password, would that matter to you?
  • Would you want to see afterward exactly what they did? Why?
  • If you could switch someone's access off with one tap, would you use it, or would that feel like distrust?
Workarounds & cost10 min
  • When your usual helper isn't around, what do you do?
  • Have you ever paid someone at a shop or centre? What was that like?
  • Has helping ever been awkward or embarrassing, for you or them?
Concept reaction & wrap10 min · light stimulus
  • Here's an idea: a trusted person helps you finish a task without seeing your secret code, and you can see and undo it. First reaction?
  • What would worry you? What would make you trust it?
  • If you had a magic wand for this, what would change? Anything I should have asked?

11Platform & visual standards

Built to Android’s grain,
finished to production polish.

These 28 screens aren’t a generic mock, they’re dressed as a real Material app and held to a production bar. Below are the Android platform patterns applied and the visual standards behind them: how touch, navigation, type, colour, motion, and error states behave, and the tokens that keep every screen consistent. The rule underneath all of it: lower the cost of every interaction for someone doing this for the very first time.

Voice-firstOne decision per screenReversible by defaultPlain, spoken languageHigh-contrast, large typeOffline-tolerant
48dp touch targets

Every actionable element meets Material’s 48×48dp minimum with 8dp spacing, sized for older hands and shared, cracked screens.

Edge-to-edge & gesture nav

Content draws under the status and nav bars with correct insets; the system back gesture is handled, and destructive back is confirmed so a mis-swipe never drops a session.

Bottom sheets, not modals

Choices (help mode, scope, helper picker) use Material bottom sheets, reachable one-handed, instead of centre dialogs that strand the thumb.

Snackbar undo

State changes surface a snackbar with a real Undo, mistakes are cheap and reversible, which matters most for anxious first-time users.

Persistent voice, no FAB

We deliberately drop the floating action button for a full-width, labelled voice bar, discoverable, describable by TalkBack, and impossible to miss.

Dynamic type & TalkBack

Layouts reflow up to the largest system font; every control has a TalkBack label and logical focus order, tested against Android accessibility scanner.

One decision per screen

Each screen asks for a single thing, no dense, multi-field forms. Progress is linear and obvious, which keeps a first-time user oriented instead of overwhelmed.

Plain, spoken language

Copy is short, verb-first, and readable aloud, in regional languages throughout, no English-only labels, no jargon, nothing that needs decoding.

Colour with meaning

Magenta is reserved for the beneficiary’s own critical actions and focus states; earthy neutrals carry ~90% of the UI, so the moments that matter actually stand out.

Offline-first & low-bandwidth

Screens degrade gracefully on 2G and dropped connections, and the voice / IVR path is a first-class fallback, not an afterthought bolted on at the end.

12Accessibility & inclusion

Designed for the person most systems leave out.

Accessibility here isn’t a compliance pass at the end, it’s the starting condition. The target user may be elderly, have low vision, read little or not at all, and share one phone. Each decision below is paired with who it serves.

Voice-first, in-language

Every screen can be spoken and heard, in the user’s own language.

Serves: non-readers & low-literacy users
7.2:1 contrast, 16sp+ type

Text clears WCAG AAA contrast and scales with the system font size.

Serves: low-vision & ageing eyes
48dp targets, one-handed reach

Controls sit in the thumb zone; bottom sheets, never stranded centre dialogs.

Serves: tremor, arthritis, first-time touch
One decision per screen

Plain, spoken language; a single clear choice at a time, no jargon.

Serves: cognitive load & anxiety
TalkBack & dynamic type

Screen-reader labels on every control; layouts hold at the largest type setting.

Serves: blind & screen-reader users
Kiosk: tactile & reach-band

A raised dot on the 5 key, controls in the 15, 48″ reach band, no rushing countdown.

Serves: seated, standing & non-reading users
Why it leads, not follows

For a Tier 3, low-literacy, shared-device audience, the accessible path is the product, not a variant of it. If voice, contrast, and reach fail, there is no fallback the user can reach on their own.

She got help without giving herself away, the task got done, and her security and autonomy stayed intact.

17Measuring success

How we would know it worked.

This is a concept, so these are the targets I would hold it to, not results. One North-Star metric that captures the core promise, the supporting signals that would prove it, and the single guardrail that must never move the wrong way. Every number is a hypothesis to test, framed against a realistic Tier 3 baseline.

North-Star
Tasks completed with help, without the credential ever leaving the beneficiary.

The whole point of Yakeen in one measure: real tasks finished by a helper, where the OTP or PIN was entered only by the beneficiary and never seen by anyone else. If this number is high, the product is doing the one thing nothing else does. If it is low, the safeguard is being bypassed, and we would rather know.

Target: ≥ 70% of assisted tasks, measured against a 0% baseline (no comparable product exists today)

Supporting signals

Effectiveness
≥ 85%
Task-completion rate

Assisted tasks that reach a confirmed receipt, first attempt. Baseline: many give up unaided or wait days for a relative.

Efficiency
< 8 min
Median time to complete

Speak-to-receipt for a typical scheme task. The safeguard must not make help so slow that people abandon it for “just hand me the phone.”

Trust
≥ 30%
Undo / revoke usage

Sessions where the beneficiary uses live-undo or revoke at least once. Counter-intuitively, higher is healthier, it means control is understood and used, not decorative.

Comprehension
≥ 75%
Consent recall at 60s

Users who can restate what they approved one minute later. The real test of informed consent, straight from the pressure-test plan, approving is not understanding.

Reach
≥ 90%
Voice-intent success

Spoken tasks correctly understood on the real dialects, ages, and audio of the target group, not lab Hindi on a good mic. The entry point has to work for the long tail.

Adoption
≥ 40%
Return-within-30-days

Beneficiaries who come back for a second task. A one-time novelty is a failure; durable use means the model earned trust.

The one guardrail, must never rise Credential-exposure rate: as close to 0% as instrumentation allows.

Any session where the helper could have seen the OTP, a shared-device bypass, a screenshot, a hand-over, is a failure of the core promise, no matter how good the other numbers look. If effectiveness climbs while this climbs too, we are winning adoption by quietly abandoning the safeguard. This metric has veto power over the North-Star.

Measured through instrumented sessions, moderated usability tests in-language, and field observation at kiosks, triangulated, because self-report alone hides exactly the coercion this product exists to prevent.

18Pressure test

The hard questions,
where this could fail.

A concept this confident deserves honest scrutiny. Below: how it could be abused, a look ahead at how it might fail, the contradictions it can’t simply design away, and the assumptions to test before a line of production code.

CoercionThe boundary is software; the room is not.

A helper sitting beside the beneficiary can still say “just give me the phone.” No consent flow stops physical, social, or economic pressure. The firewall guards against remote misuse, not the person next to you, who is exactly who people share with.

Shared deviceThe core mechanism assumes two devices. 73% have one.

“Route the OTP only to the beneficiary’s phone” is meaningless when the helper is holding that same phone. On one shared device the credential firewall is largely theatre unless we can force a second factor the user doesn’t have.

Trusted-insider fraudMost abuse is by the trusted.

Elder financial abuse is overwhelmingly committed by family and known helpers, the exact people this product tells you to choose. “Pick someone you trust” can quietly launder abuse as consent.

Comprehension gapApproving is not understanding.

Voice lowers the barrier to operate, not to grasp consequences. A confident “haan, theek hai” to an approval prompt is not informed consent, and a polished UI can make a coerced tap feel legitimate.

Dormant auditLogs are a comfort, not a control.

A tamper-evident history only protects people who review it or have someone to escalate to. For an isolated elderly user, the trail is evidence after the harm, not prevention.

Weak-language ASRVoice-first fails the long tail.

Speech recognition for low-resource languages, dialects, and elderly or accented speech is unreliable. The most excluded users get the worst recognition, the opposite of the intent.

A look ahead · it’s 18 months on and Yakeen has failed. Why?

Assume the launch happened and the concept is dead. The most likely causes:

  1. The friction won. Helpers found the firewall slower than “just hand me the phone,” and beneficiaries, wanting the task done, let them bypass it. The safeguard was both the value and the reason for abandonment.
  2. Incentives fought the design. Kiosk operators paid per completion routed around consent to hit volume; the product had no answer to their economics.
  3. Regulation caught up. Banks and identity rails treat any OTP delegation as a violation; the assisted model couldn’t get sanctioned and lived in a grey zone providers wouldn’t touch.
  4. The second factor never existed. On a shared single device we could never truly keep the OTP from the helper, so the central promise quietly became unprovable.

Three contradictions we can’t design away

1
Mechanism vs reality. The firewall needs device separation; the users’ defining reality is one shared device.
2
Software boundary vs social power. We’re trying to police an in-person power dynamic with a screen overlay.
3
Autonomy vs dependence. “Keeping control” for someone who fundamentally cannot do the task alone is, by definition, partial.
4
AI vs assumption-laundering. Automating research synthesis and validation can dress our own priors up as evidence if the loop only ever confirms us.

Test these first, before building

1
The shared-device OTP problemhighest risk

Can we deliver a real second factor, voice-biometric callback, a separate PIN, IVR-side entry, on one phone? If not, the central promise must be reframed honestly.

2
In-person coercion

Field-test whether “pause & hide” actually changes helper behaviour, or is simply bypassed by handing the phone over anyway.

3
Comprehension of consent

Measure whether users can restate what they approved 60 seconds later, the real test of informed consent.

4
ASR for the actual users

Accuracy on the real dialects, ages, and audio conditions of the target group, not lab Hindi on a good mic.

5
Regulatory viability

Get one bank or scheme to say “yes, this is sanctioned” before investing in the rails.

19Reflection

What Yakeen taught me.

Yakeen started from one stubborn observation: people don’t need to hand over their phone and PIN to get help online, they’re made to. Everything here is an attempt to separate the help from the handover. This is what building it clarified, and what I’d still put to the test.

What I learned

Trust is not transfer

People trust a helper to help, not to hold their credentials. The distance between those two is the whole of Yakeen.

Voice is the interface

For this audience, spoken, regional-language interaction isn’t a feature to bolt on later, it’s the price of entry, so it shaped everything.

Autonomy is a safety feature

Live visibility, undo, and revoke aren’t niceties. On a shared device they’re what protect a person, often a woman, from quiet coercion.

Design for the watcher

The beneficiary watching every step is the trust mechanism, so the watching experience earned as much care as the doing.

Meet help where it lives

The kiosk and the family member already exist. Yakeen formalises those behaviours instead of trying to replace them.

Make the honest version

Naming the failure modes, coercion, insider fraud, weak-language ASR, up front made the design sturdier, not weaker.

What I’d validate next

Honesty over polish, these are the assumptions most likely to break Yakeen, and the first things I’d test with real people.

  1. Shared-device OTP as a real second factor. The highest-risk bet: if the beneficiary’s “private” code step happens on a device others can see, does it actually stay private? This needs hardware-token and trusted-execution testing before anything else.
  2. Speech recognition in low-resource languages and dialects. Voice is the entry point, so ASR accuracy for accented, code-mixed, and regional speech is make-or-break, and the fallback when it fails must be graceful.
  3. Whether scoped, time-boxed grants are truly understood. “This task only, for now” is elegant on paper; first-time users need to grasp it without a manual, or the safety model is theatre.
Yakeen, in one line

You can be helped, without being handed over.
